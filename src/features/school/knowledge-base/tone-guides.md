# Tone Guides by Grade Level

Register, vocabulary, and sentence complexity should shift with the grade
band the student specifies. Default to middle-school register if no grade
is given — it's the safest legibility target for a student who didn't
specify.

## Elementary (grades 3-5)

- Short sentences, mostly one idea per sentence. Avoid nested clauses.
- Concrete nouns over abstractions. "The main character feels sad"
  beats "the protagonist experiences a sense of melancholy."
- Define any term that isn't everyday vocabulary, right where it's used —
  don't assume a glossary gets read.
- Encouraging, plain-spoken framing. No irony, no rhetorical questions
  that assume adult reading habits.
- Practice questions should be answerable by pointing to a specific line
  or fact, not requiring multi-step inference.

## Middle school (grades 6-8)

- Mix of short and medium sentences. This is where compound sentences
  ("X happened, and because of that, Y changed") start being useful.
- Introduce subject-specific vocabulary but pair it with a plain-language
  restatement the first time it appears.
- Structure matters more here — headers, numbered steps, bolded key terms
  — because students are learning to study from structure, not just prose.
  study skills, not just content, so structure should be prominent.
- Practice questions can ask "why" and "how," not just "what," but should
  still map to material explicitly covered.

## High school (grades 9-12)

- Full range of sentence complexity is fine, including subordinate
  clauses and varied openers — but still edit for clarity over cleverness.
- Vocabulary can include real subject terminology (thesis, symbolism,
  stoichiometry, etc.) without hand-holding definitions, unless the
  student is clearly a beginner in the topic.
- Analysis should ask students to connect evidence to argument, not just
  recall facts — "what does this quote reveal about the character's
  motivation," not "what did the character say."
- It's fine to note ambiguity or competing interpretations in literature
  or history — high schoolers benefit from seeing that not everything has
  one clean answer.

## College / adult learner

- Assume fluency with academic register; don't over-explain structure.
- Vocabulary can be fully technical/field-specific.
- Favor precision and economy over elaboration — a college study guide
  should read like a sharp set of lecture notes, not a textbook chapter.
- Practice questions can require synthesis across multiple sources or
  concepts, not just recall from one text.

## Across all levels

- Never talk down. "Talking simply" and "talking down" are different —
  the first shortens sentences and picks concrete words; the second adds
  a condescending tone. Only do the first.
- Match tone to the subject: a history study guide can be more narrative;
  a math or science guide should be more procedural and stepwise.
